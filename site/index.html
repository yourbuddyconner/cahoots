What you get

What members can do.

Chat

A group chat only your circle can read. Keys came from the room, rotate on their own, and live on your card.

Log in

Your circle is an authorized_keys file. SSH and git signing from the card, with a button press per login.

Tunnel

WireGuard between members, keyed from the pad. No account, no coordination server.

Seal

Files and messages sealed to one member or the whole circle. HPKE, age, TLS 1.3 all have a slot for a shared secret; the pad fills it.

Sign

Approvals, grants and facts signed by a key that only exists in silicon, checkable by anyone against the chip maker's certificate.

How you get in

Five minutes, in person.

  1. Meet. A group meets in a room, each person with a phone or a card.
  2. Draw. Each device makes random bytes from its own hardware and commits to them before it sees anyone else's.
  3. Swap. The devices pass their bytes along a pile of stacked cards or as animated codes on screen, each one encrypted to the other members, so a camera in the room learns nothing.
  4. Mix. Every device combines all the contributions into one shared pad, which is random if even one member was honest.
  5. Sign. Every device signs the list of seats with a key it made for this circle, so the circle has a signed roster.
  6. Say the words. Everyone reads six check words aloud, and if they match, every device holds the same pad.

The card

A card you keep in your wallet.

V1 is a 54 by 86 mm board with a 1.54 inch e-paper, two buttons, a coin cell, and a magnetic connector with contacts at one end, so cards stack face to face into a pile for a ceremony. Five are quoted and about to be ordered. It carries an NXP SE051 that holds the keys and attests that they were made inside it. being built

At rest it shows a sigil, a picture drawn from its own randomness, so people know each other's card across a room, and a second page is its contact card, with a code a phone camera reads. being built

V2 is the card around the Baochip, designed once V1 has been handled.

The feed

Post anywhere. Only your circle can read it.

During the Cold War, radio stations read out strings of numbers that anyone could hear and only the holder of the matching code sheet could read. Cahoots posts to a public feed the same way: anyone can see the messages, only your circle can read them or post as you, and the feed keeps them in an order nobody can quietly rewrite.
